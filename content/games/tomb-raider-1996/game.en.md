Released in October 1996 by Core Design and published by Eidos Interactive, Tomb Raider arrived at a moment when 3D hardware had just made a certain kind of game newly possible: one built not around speed or combat, but around the silence of ancient spaces and the precision of movement through them.

The game introduced Lara Croft — archaeologist, adventurer, self-sufficient explorer — as the player character and protagonist. She was designed by Toby Gard as someone whose competence was the premise of the entire game. There was no companion, no rescue narrative, no support structure. Just Lara, a pistol in each hand, and a ruin that needed reading.

The engine Core Design built for the game divided its world into interconnected rooms, rendering only what was visible through each doorway. Lighting was baked into the geometry at compile time, producing deep shadows and warm stone surfaces at no runtime cost. The result was environments that felt deliberately built rather than generically rendered.

The movement system was designed on a grid. Every jump, every ledge, every shimmy distance was a fixed unit — level geometry and character capability were co-designed as a single puzzle system. Knowing what Lara could and could not reach was the game's primary cognitive demand.

Tomb Raider sold over seven million copies and launched one of gaming's most enduring franchises. It demonstrated that a third-person game about exploring ancient ruins — quiet, precise, architecturally driven — could be a mainstream blockbuster.

## Story

Lara Croft is hired by Jacqueline Natla, CEO of Natla Technologies, to recover a fragment of the Scion — an ancient artifact of unknown origin — from ruins in Peru. The job is presented as a simple retrieval contract.

It does not stay simple.

The Scion is split into three pieces, each hidden in a different ancient civilization. Lara traces them across Peru, Greece, and Egypt, navigating tombs named after Atlantean rulers — Qualopec in the Peruvian mountains, Tihocan beneath a flooded Greek cistern — before reaching the third fragment in an Egyptian sanctuary. When the three pieces combine, they reveal a truth Natla had withheld: she is not human. She is one of three rulers of ancient Atlantis, imprisoned for conducting experiments her co-rulers deemed genocidal, freed in the 20th century and hiding behind a corporate identity.

Natla's plan has been to use the reassembled Scion as a biological catalyst — a device to breed a new generation of Atlanteans. She has been running the entire operation, using Lara, using the mercenaries Larson and Pierre, and using the ruins themselves as instruments of a much older ambition.

Lara follows her to Atlantis — a physical structure beneath the ocean, bio-organic and alien after the limestone and marble of everything before it. She destroys the breeding chamber, confronts Natla in her full Atlantean form, destroys the Scion, and escapes as the structure collapses.

The ending implies Natla survives.

The story's power is less in its plot than in its architecture. Each location is a chapter in a widening conspiracy, and the transition from ancient ruins into a living biological nightmare is one of the most effective tonal escalations in early 3D gaming.

## Development

Tomb Raider was developed by a small team at Core Design in Derby, England — estimated at six to eight people for the majority of production. The project began around 1994 and targeted the PlayStation and Sega Saturn, two platforms that had just made fully realized 3D environments commercially viable.

The central creative force was Toby Gard, a junior designer at Core Design who conceived the game, designed Lara Croft, and built its movement system. Gard's starting point was not a genre template but a question: what could a character do in 3D space that felt genuinely athletic? The answer became the entire game — a system of grids and distances where every ledge height and jump length was designed around exactly what Lara could reach.

The character went through iterations before Lara Croft. An early version was a South American woman named Laura Cruz. Eidos Interactive's marketing team requested a name change before release, judging the original too foreign for their target markets. Gard complied. He left Core Design shortly after the game shipped, uncomfortable with the direction Eidos was taking the character commercially.

The engine Core Design built was proprietary and purpose-designed. Its room-based portal rendering system — drawing only what was visible from the player's current position — was the solution to the PlayStation's lack of a hardware depth buffer. Pre-calculated static lighting gave every environment its characteristic look: warm stone, deep shadow, no dynamic changes during play.

Nathan McCree composed the score, including the short discovery sting that has persisted across every subsequent Tomb Raider game. The audio philosophy was restraint: long passages of silence broken by ambient sound, with music used only as an exclamation — on discovery, on encounter, on revelation.

## Legacy

Tomb Raider's influence on the action-adventure genre was immediate and lasting. Its combination of third-person traversal, environmental puzzles, and atmospheric ancient spaces became a template that defined a generation of games. Prince of Persia: The Sands of Time (2003) refined the traversal model; Uncharted (2007) translated the formula into cinematic language; the Tomb Raider franchise itself was reinvented twice in the decades that followed.

Lara Croft's cultural significance extended beyond sales figures. She was one of the first female protagonists to anchor a mainstream action franchise, and her commercial success challenged prevailing assumptions about the demographics of game audiences. The conversation she provoked — about how female characters are designed, marketed, and owned — has not resolved and remains active.

Toby Gard returned to the character he created for Tomb Raider: Legend in 2006, working with Crystal Dynamics on Lara's reinvention for a new generation. The 2013 reboot revisited her origins entirely, from a different creative and commercial context but with the same fundamental question Gard had asked in 1994: who is Lara Croft, and what is she capable of?

The 1996 game's answer — a capable, solitary, archaeologist-adventurer who moves through ancient spaces with precision and purpose — has proven more durable than any of its sequels' revisions.

## Trivia

- Lara Croft was designed by Toby Gard, who was a junior designer at Core Design at the time of the game's development.
- The character's original name was Laura Cruz before Eidos marketing requested a change to a more British-sounding name.
- The movement system was designed on a grid — every jump and traversal action moves Lara a fixed unit distance, allowing levels to be built precisely around her capabilities.
- Console versions used Save Crystals rather than free saving; the PC version allowed unlimited saving, creating a significant difficulty difference between platforms.
- The Tyrannosaurus Rex in the Lost Valley cannot be defeated within a typical ammunition budget — avoidance is the intended approach for most players.
- The Midas Touch puzzle is the game's most famous trap: placing a lead bar on the hand transforms it into gold, but sending Lara onto it directly kills her instantly.
- Nathan McCree composed the score, including the discovery sting that has appeared in modified form in Tomb Raider games for thirty years.
- Toby Gard left Core Design shortly after the game's release and returned to the franchise twelve years later for Tomb Raider: Legend (2006).
- Jacqueline Natla is one of the franchise's few recurring villains, reappearing in Tomb Raider: Anniversary (2007).
- The game's four locations — Peru, Greece, Egypt, Atlantis — each serve as a chapter in a widening conspiracy, with Atlantis representing a deliberate tonal break from everything that preceded it.
