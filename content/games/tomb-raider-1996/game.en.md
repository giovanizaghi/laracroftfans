Tomb Raider is the 1996 single-player action-adventure developed by Core Design and originally published by Eidos Interactive. The first commercial version launched for Sega Saturn in Europe on October 25, followed by PlayStation and MS-DOS releases in November. It is the game that introduced Lara Croft, the Scion, Jacqueline Natla, and the series' combination of third-person exploration, grid-based traversal, environmental puzzles, combat, and secrets.

The campaign contains 15 levels divided among Peru, Greece, Egypt, and the lost island that holds the Great Pyramid of Atlantis. A separate training area, identified by the original PC manual as “Lara's Home,” teaches the movement system. This entry concerns the game released in 1996. It does not treat Tomb Raider: Anniversary (2007), Tomb Raider I-III Remastered (2024), or later continuity additions as if they appeared in the original.

## Story

In 1945, the Trinity nuclear test at Los Alamos, New Mexico, dislodges the seal of an ancient prison and releases its occupant. The game does not identify her immediately. Decades later, in 1996, Lara Croft is approached by the mercenary Larson at the Imperial Hotel in Calcutta. He introduces her to Jacqueline Natla, the head of Natla Technologies, who hires Lara to recover an ancient object from the tomb of Qualopec in Peru. Lara is uninterested in the money but accepts the challenge “for sport.”

After her guide is killed by wolves in the Andes, Lara enters the ruins alone. She passes through caves, the City of Vilcabamba, and a valley inhabited by surviving dinosaurs before reaching Qualopec's tomb and taking one piece of the Scion. Larson attempts to seize it. Lara defeats him without killing him and learns that Natla has sent another raider, Pierre DuPont, after a second piece.

Lara breaks into Natla Technologies in Seattle and finds the diary of Brother Herbert, a monk who wrote in 1573 that the Atlantean ruler Tihocan had been buried beneath St. Francis' Folly with his part of the Scion. In Greece, Lara follows Pierre through the Folly, the Colosseum, Palace Midas, and the Cistern. She kills Pierre in the Tomb of Tihocan and recovers the second piece. When joined, the fragments show her the location of a third piece in Egypt.

Lara explores the City and Obelisk of Khamoon and reaches the Sanctuary of the Scion. She kills Larson after he confronts her again and claims the last fragment. The assembled artifact gives her a vision of ancient Atlantis: Natla was one of its three rulers, alongside Qualopec and Tihocan, but broke their shared bond, took the Scion, and used the Great Pyramid to pursue forced evolution through monstrous creations. Her peers imprisoned her in suspended animation. The 1945 test seen at the beginning of the game set her free.

Natla and three armed followers ambush Lara, take the Scion, and leave her for dead. Lara escapes, pursues them by motorcycle, and boards Natla's yacht. It carries her to a remote island where Natla Technologies has excavated the Great Pyramid. In the mines Lara recovers her weapons and defeats the Cowboy, the Bald Man, and the Skateboard Kid—descriptive names used for figures who are not given personal names in the original English release.

Inside the pyramid, the architecture changes from rock and machinery to pulsing organic chambers filled with Atlantean mutants. Natla activates the structure with the Scion to create a new breed. Lara confronts her, survives the Pyramid's giant legless creation, destroys the Scion, defeats Natla after the Atlantean revives during their battle, and escapes the collapsing island by boat. The 1996 ending does not show Natla surviving her final defeat.

## Gameplay

Tomb Raider is played from a third-person perspective. Lara runs, walks, turns, sidesteps, rolls, jumps in several directions, grabs and shimmies along ledges, swims on and below the surface, pushes or pulls blocks, operates switches, and uses keys or puzzle objects. Walking prevents her from stepping accidentally over an edge. Holding the action command during a jump allows her to catch a reachable ledge.

The environments and Lara's movement were designed around a block grid. Jump distances and ledge heights are therefore consistent enough to be measured by the player. The original “tank” controls move Lara relative to the direction she faces, while the Look command allows the surrounding room or a landing point to be inspected. Traversal is itself a puzzle: progress depends on reading the architecture and understanding which sequence of movements the geometry permits.

Combat uses automatic target locking. Lara begins with dual pistols and unlimited ammunition; the shotgun, dual Magnums, and dual Uzis can be found later and use finite pickups. Small and large medipacks restore health. The inventory is presented as rotating menu rings and also stores keys, lead and gold bars, fuses, ankhs, scarabs, and other level-specific objects.

There are 45 secrets across the 15 campaign levels. A short chime confirms a secret, and end-of-level statistics record progress. In some original releases the third secret in The Great Pyramid can be collected without registering, a known shipped bug. Saving also differs by platform: the Saturn and PlayStation versions use fixed, single-use Save Crystals, while the MS-DOS version allows saving from the passport at any point.

## Development

The surviving design documents begin in December 1994, and the finished game shipped in October 1996. Later accounts commonly describe approximately 18 months of full production after an initial period of concept work. Toby Gard originated the concept and designed Lara, while lead programmer Paul Douglas built the custom technology with the rest of the programming team. Gard said in a 1998 interview that the core team numbered six: Gard, Douglas, programmers Gavin Rummery and Jason Gosling, and graphic artists and level designers Heather Gibson and Neal Boyd.

The original credits are more precise than many later summaries. They name Douglas as lead programmer; Gard as lead graphic artist and original concept creator; Gosling and Rummery as programmers; Gibson and Boyd as graphic artists; Vicky Arnold as scriptwriter; Nathan McCree as composer; Martin Iveson for sound effects; and Jeremy H. Smith—Jeremy Heath-Smith—as executive producer. Additional programming and artwork were supplied by several other Core Design staff. Shelley Blond voiced Lara in the English release, and McCree voiced Brother Herbert.

Core Design built a proprietary 3D engine because its earlier technology could not support the required movement and layered spaces. Rooms were assembled with a grid-based editor, making character dimensions, jumps, ledges, slopes, doors, water volumes, movable blocks, and triggers part of a common spatial system. Prince of Persia was an acknowledged influence on Gard's interest in believable character movement, but Tomb Raider applied that concern to polygonal rooms navigable in three dimensions.

Vicky Arnold turned the early premise and separate locations into the shipped story, finalized Lara's biography for promotional material, and created Jacqueline Natla. Heather Gibson designed eight of the finished levels, including Lara's Home and Caves, according to the official franchise retrospective. McCree's music is used selectively rather than as a continuous score, leaving long sections to environmental sound before short cues mark danger, discovery, or revelation.

Early documents are evidence of development, not a script for the final game. They contain discarded creatures, areas, jokes, character details, and spellings. The May 1996 document calls the protagonist “Lara Cruz,” while a modern official article calls the early version “Laura Cruz.” Both prove that the Cruz surname preceded Croft, but they do not support pretending that every draft detail is part of the released story.

## Legacy

Contemporary reviews praised the scale of the 3D environments, the range of Lara's movement, and the mixture of action with architectural problem-solving. GameSpot described it at the time as a new benchmark for 32-bit 3D games. Metacritic's later collection of 13 PlayStation reviews produces a score of 91; that number is a retrospective aggregate, not a score that existed at launch.

Later sales summaries commonly state that the game sold more than seven million copies worldwide, although a clearly accessible original Eidos statement for that exact total is difficult to trace. What is beyond dispute is that its success produced an annualized sequel series, made Lara Croft a widely recognized character beyond games, and established the franchise's core vocabulary of tombs, traversal, puzzles, secrets, wildlife, and supernatural artifacts.

Tomb Raider: Unfinished Business added four PC bonus levels in 1997 and was later packaged with the original as Tomb Raider Gold. Tomb Raider: Anniversary rebuilt the Scion story in 2007 with different mechanics, characterization, and continuity; it is a reimagining, not a patched edition of the 1996 game. Tomb Raider I-III Remastered included the original game and Unfinished Business in 2024 with switchable graphics, optional modern controls, and quality-of-life changes while retaining the classic level structure.

## Characters

Lara Croft — The player character. The original manual presents her as the aristocratic daughter of Lord Henshingly Croft, changed by surviving a Himalayan plane crash and funding later expeditions through books and journals. These biography details come from the manual, not dialogue in the campaign. The game never states an exact birth date or says that she is 21 during the Scion expedition.

Jacqueline Natla — Head of Natla Technologies and the principal antagonist. She is an ancient Atlantean ruler freed by the 1945 Trinity test. She employs Lara and rival raiders to recover the Scion, then uses it to reactivate the Great Pyramid and continue her program of forced evolution.

Larson — Natla's American mercenary and Lara's prior acquaintance. He introduces the contract in Calcutta, survives Lara's first attack in Peru, and is killed when he opposes her in Egypt. “Larson Conway” is a later expanded name; the 1996 English game calls him Larson.

Pierre DuPont — A French rival hired to find Tihocan's Scion fragment. His full surname is spoken by Larson in the original game. Pierre repeatedly ambushes Lara through Greece and dies in the Tomb of Tihocan.

Qualopec and Tihocan — Two of the three rulers of Atlantis. They condemn Natla and divide the Scion. Qualopec is associated with Peru and Tihocan with Greece; their tombs preserve two fragments of the artifact.

Brother Herbert — A monk whose 1573 diary identifies the tomb of Tihocan beneath St. Francis' Folly. He is heard only through Lara's reading of the document.

The Peruvian guide — He leads Lara to the cave entrance and is killed by wolves. A surviving script names him Carlos, but the released scene never speaks his name.

The Cowboy, the Bald Man, and the Skateboard Kid — Natla's three armed followers, described rather than personally named in the English release. Names or biographies supplied by Anniversary and later reference material should not be backdated to 1996.

Atlantean Doppelgänger and Natla's Creation — Two distinctive pyramid creatures. The skinless Doppelgänger mirrors Lara's movement; “Bacon Lara” is a fan nickname. Natla's Creation is the giant legless mutant fought at the beginning of the final level.

## Locations

Lara's Home — An optional training space selected from the main menu. The original manual uses this label; “Croft Manor” became the standard series name.

Calcutta and Seattle — Story locations shown in cinematics. The Imperial Hotel meeting begins the contract, and Natla Technologies' headquarters supplies Lara with the clue leading to Greece.

Peru — Caves, Vilcabamba, the Lost Valley, and the Tomb of Qualopec. This chapter introduces the movement system and culminates in the first Scion fragment.

Greece — St. Francis' Folly, the Colosseum, Palace Midas, the Cistern, and the Tomb of Tihocan. The region centers on vertical navigation, mythological rooms, water management, and the second fragment.

Egypt — The City of Khamoon, Obelisk of Khamoon, and Sanctuary of the Scion. Lara obtains the final fragment and sees the history of the Atlantean rulers.

Lost island and the Great Pyramid — Natla's Mines form the industrial approach to the buried Atlantean structure. Atlantis and The Great Pyramid replace conventional ruins with machinery, lava, organic walls, incubation pods, and mutants.

## Fact Check

- Confirmed — The original retail title is Tomb Raider. “Tomb Raider I” and “TR1” are later shorthand used to distinguish it from sequels and the 2013 reboot.
- Confirmed — The first commercial release was the European Sega Saturn version on October 25, 1996. PlayStation and MS-DOS editions followed in November.
- Confirmed — The campaign has 15 levels plus Lara's Home as a separate training area, and the campaign contains 45 secrets.
- Confirmed — Lara's pistols have unlimited ammunition. The Tyrannosaurus rex can therefore be killed; avoiding it is a player choice, not an ammunition requirement.
- Confirmed with source distinction — The manual gives Lara a pre-game Himalayan survival biography but no birth date. Its chronology does not make her 21 in 1996; 21 is the age at which it says she left finishing school.
- Source conflict — A May 1996 Core Design document says “Lara Cruz,” while a 2024 official article says “Laura Cruz.” The early surname Cruz is certain; the exact first-name spelling should be attributed to the source.
- Myth — The claim that a mouse slip accidentally increased Lara's bust by 150 percent originated as a joke and should not be presented as a development fact.
- Confirmed — Lara has no animated braid or ponytail on the playable 1996 model, although one appears in cinematics and artwork. The feature was implemented in Tomb Raider II.
- Confirmed — “Pierre DuPont” appears in original dialogue. “Larson Conway” does not; Conway is a later addition.
- Confirmed — “Bacon Lara” is a fan nickname for the Atlantean Doppelgänger, not its original in-game name.
- Confirmed — Natla's release is shown in the opening through the Trinity nuclear test. It is not an unexplained twentieth-century event.
- Unsupported — The 1996 ending does not imply that Natla survives after her final defeat. Her later appearances belong to the separate Legend/Anniversary/Underworld continuity.
- Draft only — Unused enemies, rooms, biographies, and dialogue in Core Design's design documents reveal production history but are not canon unless they appear in the shipped game or its manual.
- Qualified — “More than seven million copies” is a widely repeated lifetime-sales figure, but it should be described as a later reported total unless an original audited Eidos statement is cited.

## Trivia

- The official release date used by the franchise is October 25, 1996, corresponding to the first European Sega Saturn launch.
- Toby Gard confirmed that the core development team consisted of six people, although additional staff are named in the credits.
- The original credits list Paul Douglas as lead programmer, Toby Gard as lead graphic artist and concept creator, and Vicky Arnold as scriptwriter.
- Shelley Blond provided Lara's English voice; Nathan McCree composed the music and voiced Brother Herbert.
- Heather Gibson designed eight of the finished levels, including Lara's Home and Caves.
- The campaign contains 15 levels, with Lara's Home available separately as a training area.
- There are 45 secrets, but a shipped bug can prevent the last secret in The Great Pyramid from appearing in the statistics.
- Lara's arsenal consists of dual pistols, a shotgun, dual Magnums, and dual Uzis.
- The dual pistols have unlimited ammunition, so every enemy that can take weapon damage—including the Tyrannosaurus rex—can be defeated with them.
- Saturn and PlayStation use fixed one-use Save Crystals; MS-DOS allows saving at any point through the passport.
- Palace Midas turns lead bars into gold, while placing Lara on the hand turns her into gold and kills her.
- The opening nuclear test is the Trinity test at Los Alamos in 1945 and is the event that releases Natla.
- Pierre DuPont's surname is original to the 1996 dialogue, but Larson has no surname in the original English script.
- “Bacon Lara” is a fan-created name for the skinless Atlantean Doppelgänger.
- Tomb Raider: Unfinished Business added four bonus levels in 1997; they are an expansion, not part of the original 15-level campaign.
