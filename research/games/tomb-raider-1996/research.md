# Research: Tomb Raider (1996)

> **ARCHIVAL REVIEW — updated 2026-08-04.** This document distinguishes shipped material, original paratext, development drafts, later continuity, reported figures, and fan terminology. Public copy should preserve those distinctions.

## Status Vocabulary

| Status                   | Meaning                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `[confirmed]`            | Supported by the shipped 1996 game, original manual or credits, an official franchise source, or a direct developer interview. |
| `[documented-secondary]` | Consistent across specialist references, but not yet connected here to an accessible primary record.                           |
| `[draft-only]`           | Found in a development document but absent or changed in the shipped game. It is production history, not released canon.       |
| `[later-continuity]`     | Introduced or expanded in a later remake, reboot, remaster, or reference work.                                                 |

## Scope

This record covers the original Tomb Raider developed by Core Design and first released in 1996. It includes:

- the 15-level campaign and the separate Lara's Home training area;
- the original Sega Saturn, PlayStation, and MS-DOS editions;
- story and character information present in the shipped game;
- biography and control information in the original manual;
- original credits;
- production evidence from preserved design documents and developer interviews;
- Unfinished Business, Anniversary, and the 2024 remaster only as later related releases.

It does not merge Anniversary's rewritten characterization, names, combat, level layouts, or Legend-era continuity into the 1996 record.

### Date Corrections

- The previous archive date of October 25 is valid as the first release date.
- The previous research draft's November 1 European dates were unsupported and have been removed.
- “PC (DOS/Windows)” is misleading for the original launch. The retail PC release was MS-DOS; later patches, ports, storefront packages, and Gold editions complicate the Windows label.

### Team Size

Toby Gard stated in a 1998 Game Developer interview that Tomb Raider had a core team of six. The six generally identified are Gard, Douglas, Gavin Rummery, Jason Gosling, Heather Gibson, and Neal Boyd. `[confirmed]`

“Six-person team” describes the core production group, not every credited contributor. The original credits list additional programmers, artists, audio staff, the writer, and executive production. Public copy should avoid saying only six people worked on the game in any capacity.

## Development History

### Chronology

- December 16, 1994 — preserved game design document version 1. `[confirmed-primary-document]`
- March 20, 1995 — design document version 1.3. `[confirmed-primary-document]`
- May 9, 1996 — design document version 1.8. `[confirmed-primary-document]`
- October 25, 1996 — first commercial release. `[confirmed]`

The game is often described as having been developed in approximately 18 months. `[documented-secondary]` The surviving documents show a wider calendar span from concept documentation in late 1994 to release in late 1996. The clean formulation is “roughly 18 months of full production following initial concept work,” unless a direct production schedule is cited.

### Concept and Technology

Toby Gard is credited with the original concept and Lara Croft. Paul Douglas is credited as lead programmer. A preserved contemporary interview states that Douglas designed the 3D engine from scratch because Core Design's earlier engines were not flexible enough for the project. `[confirmed-preserved-interview]`

The official classic-era retrospective describes the original levels and controls as grid-based. Lara's dimensions and movement distances were designed together with block-based rooms. A later official Croft Manor article gives examples: Lara is approximately three-quarters of a block tall, and predictable block distances determine standing and running grabs. `[confirmed-official-retrospective]`

Prince of Persia was an acknowledged influence. Gard told Game Developer that its seamless, believable character movement was enormously influential. Tomb Raider translated that concern into navigable polygonal rooms, swimming, ledge grabs, and vertically layered spaces. `[confirmed-developer-interview]`

### Writing and Level Design

The official franchise retrospective credits Vicky Arnold with turning the separate locations into a coherent story, defining Lara's biography, writing FMV/cutscene dialogue, and creating Jacqueline Natla. `[confirmed-official-retrospective]`

The same source states that Heather Gibson designed eight levels in Tomb Raider, including Lara's Home and Caves. `[confirmed-official-retrospective]` Original credits call Gibson and Neal Boyd “Graphic Artists”; their actual responsibilities included level construction, texturing, and environmental design.

### Draft Documents Are Not Canon

The May 1996 design document is especially valuable and especially easy to misuse. It includes:

- early level groupings that differ from the 15 shipped stages;
- discarded enemies such as snakes, a giant lizard, a sea monster, and a man-eating plant;
- placeholder and joke descriptions;
- draft dialogue and staging altered before release;
- the name “Lara Cruz”;
- production terms such as “Streaky Bacon Man.”

These entries document intent at that date. They do not prove that an unused creature, biography, room, or name exists in the released fiction.

## Lara Croft: Evidence Layers

### Shipped Game

The game presents Lara as a famous archaeologist-adventurer, known to Larson, willing to accept Natla's contract for the challenge rather than the payment, technically capable, and prepared to investigate Natla independently. Her personality is communicated through sparse dialogue and action. `[confirmed]`

### Original Manual

The PC manual says Lara is the daughter of Lord Henshingly Croft, was raised as an aristocrat, left finishing school at 21, survived a plane crash in the Himalayas, reached the village of Tokakeriby two weeks later, rejected upper-class society, spent the next eight years studying ancient civilizations, was disowned, and funded travel through books and journals. `[confirmed-paratext]`

These facts are original 1996 paratext, but not dialogue in the campaign. They can be described as the original biography with source attribution.

### Age

The game and examined manual do not provide a birth date. The manual says the crash occurred after finishing school at 21 and that eight years of travel followed. It therefore does not support “Lara is 21 in Tomb Raider.” `[myth/incorrect-reading]`

Later promotional biographies supplied dates, but those should be identified as later biography material rather than dialogue from the game.

### Cruz / Croft Name

- Core Design design document version 1.8 says “Lara Cruz.” `[confirmed-primary-draft]`
- A 2024 official franchise article says the initial character was “Laura Cruz.” `[confirmed-source-conflict]`
- Both support Cruz as the early surname.
- The precise early first-name spelling is source-dependent and should not be flattened into a single unqualified fact.

### 150 Percent “Accident”

The story that a mouse slip accidentally increased Lara's bust by 150 percent traces to a joking remark and was later repeated as literal production history. It should be classified as a myth, not trivia. `[myth]`

### Hair

The playable 1996 model does not have an animated braid or ponytail, although a braid appears in FMVs and art. Tomb Raider II implemented the moving ponytail. The previous draft's claim that Lara's braid served as an in-game movement indicator in 1996 was false. `[confirmed-correction]`

## Story Record

### Shipped Plot

1. The 1945 Trinity test at Los Alamos releases a figure from an ancient chamber.
2. In 1996 at the Imperial Hotel in Calcutta, Larson introduces Lara to Jacqueline Natla of Natla Technologies.
3. Natla hires Lara to retrieve an object from Qualopec's tomb in Peru.
4. Lara crosses Caves, Vilcabamba, Lost Valley, and Tomb of Qualopec and obtains the first Scion fragment.
5. Larson tries to take it. Lara learns that Pierre DuPont is pursuing another fragment.
6. Lara breaks into Natla Technologies in Seattle and finds Brother Herbert's 1573 diary.
7. In Greece she pursues Pierre through five levels, kills him in Tomb of Tihocan, and obtains the second fragment.
8. The combined pieces reveal the third fragment in Egypt.
9. Lara crosses three Egyptian levels, kills Larson, and assembles the Scion.
10. The Scion shows Natla's ancient trial by Qualopec and Tihocan.
11. Natla and three mercenaries take the artifact.
12. Lara escapes, follows Natla to a remote island, and enters Natla's Mines.
13. Lara defeats the three mercenaries, enters the Great Pyramid, and learns that Natla intends to force a new evolutionary age.
14. Lara destroys the Scion, defeats Natla, and escapes the collapsing island by boat.

### Corrections to the Previous Draft

- Atlantis is not established as an underwater structure. The final area is a pyramid excavated within a mountain on a remote island. `[confirmed-correction]`
- Natla's release is not unexplained: it is shown in the opening nuclear-test sequence. `[confirmed-correction]`
- The game does not describe the Scion simply as a “biological catalyst.” It is an artifact of power and knowledge that activates the pyramid; “biological catalyst” was editorial inference. `[interpretation-removed]`
- Qualopec and Tihocan condemn Natla for breaking their shared bond and abusing the Scion. “They considered her experiments genocidal” is stronger than the original dialogue and should not be used without qualification. `[overstatement-removed]`
- The original ending does not imply Natla survives after the final battle. Her mid-battle revival is part of the boss encounter, not a post-ending survival reveal. `[unsupported-claim-removed]`
- Pierre is killed by Lara in the Tomb of Tihocan. The prior claim that a trap might kill him depending on player pace was false. `[confirmed-correction]`

The previous research used Larson Conway. The original English dialogue and preserved credits use Larson without a surname. The surname belongs to expanded later characterization and must not appear as an unqualified 1996 name. `[later-continuity]`

The prior draft described “human form with firearms” followed by a winged Atlantean form. That is not the 1996 boss structure. In the original, Natla attacks as a winged Atlantean firing projectiles, falls, and rises for a final ground phase. ## Gameplay Systems

### Movement

The manual documents running, walking, turning, sidestepping, rolling, directional jumping, surface and underwater swimming, vaulting, grabbing, shimming, picking up objects, switches, puzzle items, push/pull blocks, and the Look command. `[confirmed-primary-manual]`

Walking stops Lara at edges. Holding Action while jumping allows a ledge grab. The grid makes jump preparation and range legible and repeatable. `[confirmed]`

### Combat and Inventory

- Automatic target acquisition and lock. `[confirmed-manual]`
- Dual pistols from the start. `[confirmed-manual]`
- Shotgun, dual Magnums, and dual Uzis found during the campaign. `[confirmed]`
- Unlimited pistol ammunition; finite ammunition for other weapons. `[confirmed-official-retrospective/shipped-game]`
- Small medipack restores half health; large medipack restores full health according to the PC manual. `[confirmed-primary-manual]`
- Keys and puzzle pieces remain in rotating inventory rings. `[confirmed-primary-manual]`

### Tyrannosaurus Rex

The Tyrannosaurus rex can be killed. Because the pistols have unlimited ammunition, the previous claim that it cannot normally be defeated within the ammunition budget was false. Avoidance is possible, but not required by resources. `[myth-corrected]`

### Save Systems

- PlayStation and Sega Saturn — fixed, one-use Save Crystals within levels. `[confirmed-shipped-platform-behavior]`
- MS-DOS — save from the passport at any point; F5 opens the save page. `[confirmed-primary-manual]`

The previous “console versions used Save Crystals” statement was correct, but they should be described as fixed single-use save points, not inventory crystals as in Tomb Raider III.

### Camera

The camera follows Lara and uses authored camera changes at particular points. The Look command temporarily allows inspection around Lara and can reset the camera behind her. The previous statement that the camera “could not be freely rotated” was broadly accurate for normal follow behavior but incomplete because Look provides manual inspection. `[confirmed-primary-manual]`

## Engine and Technical Claims

### Safe Claims

- The engine was custom-built at Core Design. `[confirmed]`
- Level interaction was grid/block based. `[confirmed]`
- Environments support stacked vertical spaces, water, movable blocks, triggers, enemies, and polygonal characters. `[confirmed-shipped-game]`
- Platform builds shared core game logic with platform-specific work. `[confirmed-developer-account, secondary preservation]`

### Claims Removed or Qualified

- “Enemies were sprite based” — false. Shipped enemies are animated polygonal models. `[confirmed-correction]`
- “All lighting was baked at compile time” — plausible technical description but not adequately sourced in the reviewed primary material; omit from public copy until a developer or engine source is cited. `[unresolved-technical]`
- “Portal rendering was specifically a solution to PlayStation's lack of a hardware depth buffer” — the room/portal behavior is observable and documented by reverse engineering, but the asserted design rationale was not established by the primary sources reviewed. `[qualified]`
- “Atlantis uses non-Euclidean architecture” — rhetorical and technically misleading. Atlantis remains grid-based. `[interpretation-removed]`

## Music and Audio

Nathan McCree composed the score; Martin Iveson is credited for sound effects. `[confirmed-primary-credits]`

The shipped game uses music selectively. Long passages rely on ambient loops and creature/mechanical sounds, while music cues punctuate discovery, threat, or narrative moments. This description is directly observable, but claims about a formal written “audio philosophy” should be attributed to a McCree interview if quoted as intent. `[confirmed-observation; intent unresolved]`

Shelley Blond voices Lara in English. Nathan McCree voices Brother Herbert. The official 2024 retrospective also notes that Heather Gibson and Susie Hamilton contributed some exertion sounds that made it into the finished game. `[confirmed-official-retrospective]`

## Reception and Commercial Performance

### Contemporary Reception

GameSpot's 1996 review praised the game as a new benchmark for 32-bit 3D gaming and emphasized its balance of action and strategy. `[confirmed-contemporary-review]`

Metacritic currently reports a PlayStation score of 91 based on 13 reviews. `[confirmed-current-aggregate]` This is a retrospective aggregation of older criticism; Metacritic did not provide this score in 1996.

### Sales

“More than seven million copies worldwide” is widely repeated in later reference works. `[documented-secondary]` The reviewed sources did not yield an accessible original Eidos financial statement establishing the exact lifetime total. Public copy should say “later sales summaries report more than seven million” rather than presenting the number as an audited figure.

### Cultural Impact

The game unquestionably produced a long-running franchise and made Lara Croft a character recognized beyond the game itself. Claims such as “first successful female game protagonist” should be avoided: earlier female protagonists existed, and “first” depends on definitions. A defensible formulation is that Lara became one of the first female protagonists to lead a globally successful 3D action franchise. `[qualified-historical]`

## Related Releases

### Tomb Raider: Unfinished Business / Tomb Raider Gold

Four bonus PC levels were released in 1997 as Unfinished Business and later packaged with the original. They are not part of the original 15-level campaign:

- Return to Egypt
- Temple of the Cat
- Atlantean Stronghold
- The Hive

`[confirmed]`

### Tomb Raider: Anniversary (2007)

Anniversary is a reimagining of the Scion expedition using Legend-era mechanics and a rewritten version of the story. Its names, motivations, biographies, boss designs, and continuity should not be silently imported into the 1996 entry. `[later-continuity]`

### Tomb Raider I-III Remastered (2024)

The Aspyr/Crystal Dynamics collection includes Tomb Raider and Unfinished Business with optional remastered graphics, classic or modern controls, achievements, photo mode, and other quality-of-life changes. Its base campaign preserves the original level structure, but remaster-only presentation and mechanics should be labeled accordingly. `[confirmed-official]`

## Editorial Guidance

Public copy should:

- call the game Tomb Raider and qualify “TR1” as shorthand;
- keep the first release date at October 25, 1996;
- call the original PC platform MS-DOS when discussing the 1996 release;
- identify Eidos Interactive as original publisher and Crystal Dynamics as the current franchise owner/publisher only when relevant;
- distinguish shipped game, manual biography, drafts, later continuity, and fan terms;
- use Larson without a surname and Pierre DuPont with the original surname;
- describe the guide as unnamed on screen, noting Carlos only when discussing the script;
- avoid an exact age for Lara;
- avoid claiming Natla survives;
- avoid the 150 percent mouse-slip story except as a debunked myth;
- avoid presenting the sales total as audited without a primary financial source;
- keep Unfinished Business outside the 15-level base campaign.
